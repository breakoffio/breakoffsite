## Rank #1: France banned social media for under-15s and forgot to say how

**Why it ranks first:** It is the only perishable thing in this brief, the ban went live on 9/1 so it is nine days old and still current, and last week's brief already pre-committed to ranking it now. More importantly, the correction above turned it from a third "country bans thing" post into an actual argument. The law has no verification mechanism in it. That is the story, and most of the wire coverage did not lead with it.

It is clear of what we have published. `/blog/australia-teen-social-media-ban-lessons/` was about outcome data three months in. `/blog/uk-social-media-curfew-teens/` was about defaults as policy. This one is about enforcement, or the absence of it, plus the school phone clause nobody covered. It also has zero overlap with the queued ChatGPT piece, which matters this week: if that post gets rescued and runs later, this one does not cannibalise it. Safe to auto-publish.

**Suggested title:** France Banned Social Media for Under-15s. Nobody Wrote Down How.

**Key points to cover:**

- What is actually in force. Parliament passed it 7/21. From 9/1/26 no new accounts for under-15s on TikTok, Instagram, Facebook, Snapchat or X. From January 2027 existing under-15 accounts must be age-verified or closed. No parental consent exception, which is the loophole critics said would sink Australia's version. Online encyclopedias, educational and scientific directories and open-source development platforms are exempt.
- The hole in the middle. The law says under-15s cannot hold accounts. It does not say how a platform establishes that anyone is 15. That question was handed to CNIL, the privacy regulator, which has to approve whatever tools platforms propose. So the deadline arrived before the method did. Write this straight and without sneering. It is a genuinely hard problem: every workable age check trades a child's privacy for a child's protection, and the French parliament chose to pass the law and let someone else make that trade later. That is a real decision, not an oversight, and it is worth saying out loud.
- The half of the law nobody covered. The same bill bans phones in secondary schools. France has had a ban in primary and middle schools since 2018. This extends it to lycée. For a 16-year-old that is a bigger daily change than the account ban is for a 14-year-old, and it barely made the coverage. Link once to `/blog/phone-free-schools-51-percent-drop/` and move on.
- The close, which is our usual point but earn it rather than asserting it. Australia, the UK, France, with Chile and Argentina moving. Four governments have now decided that the answer to a designed compulsion is an enforced default that the user cannot switch off. That is a real finding, and it happens to be the same one the blocker research pointed at in `/blog/do-app-blockers-work-research/`. Every one of these laws also draws its line at a birthday and exempts the adults who wrote it. Keep this to a paragraph. We have made the asymmetry point three times now and it is starting to sound like a tic.

**Hot take:** France passed a law that says children may not have social media accounts and then declined to say how anyone would know. That is not incompetence. That is a parliament discovering, in public, that you cannot check a fifteen-year-old's age without inspecting everybody, and deciding to ship the headline and let the privacy regulator absorb the part that makes people angry. Every country doing this hits the same wall, and the wall is that verifying a child requires surveilling an adult. Meanwhile the actual enforcement mechanism in a French teenager's life is not CNIL. It is that their lycée now takes the phone at the door. That clause was in the same bill, it is the part that will change a real Tuesday, and it got a fraction of the coverage because nobody can argue about it on television.

## Rank #2: iOS 27's best parental control asks apps to grade themselves

**Why it ranks second:** This is the most original thing we found this week and it is the one where we have first-hand standing, because we are a developer who has to answer this questionnaire. It ranks second and not first for two reasons. It is not perishable in the way France is, and it sits close enough to the queued ChatGPT piece that running both is a sequencing question. If the ChatGPT post gets rescued, run it first. This is the sequel to it, not a substitute.

**Adjacency warning for the writer:** `/blog/apple-screen-time-loophole-app-categories/` already covered Apple sorting apps into Social, Entertainment, Games and Other. Do not rewrite it. The new fact is that the sorting is now partly a self-declaration by the developer, with a documented way to opt out of the Social Media bucket. That is different and it is the whole piece. Also do not write this as an Apple hit job. The questionnaire is a reasonable thing for Apple to do and it is more honest than an algorithm guessing. The point is what it depends on.

**Suggested title:** iOS 27 Lets Apps Decide Whether They Count as Social Media

**Key points to cover:**

- What changed and when. Apple added social media questions to the App Store Connect age rating questionnaire on 7/9/26. From September they are mandatory for new apps and for every update. Declare social media capability, defined as redistributing, amplifying or interacting with user-generated content through a feed or similar discovery mechanism, and you get a minimum 13+ rating, a Social Media descriptor on your App Store page, and inclusion in the Social Media Time Allowance category in iOS 27.
- The opt-out, quoted accurately from Apple's own developer note. If you declare social media capability but it is disabled for anyone under 13, your app is not included in the Social Media Time Allowance for under-13 users, provided you adopt the Declared Age Range API. Say plainly that this is a sensible carve-out with an obvious second use: a compliant path out of the bucket for any app willing to gate its feed for younger users.
- Why the incentive runs one way. A 13+ minimum rating and a Social Media label cost you installs. Landing in a category cap means a parent's single setting can throttle you. Nobody has to lie for this to bend. The definition has edges, and every product team on a borderline case will read those edges in the direction that is cheaper. That is not cynicism about developers, it is what happens when you attach a penalty to a self-report.
- What it means for a parent, which is the part the tech coverage skips. Time Allowances are the headline feature of iOS 27 Screen Time and they are genuinely good. But a category cap is only as good as the category, and the category is now partly downstream of a form. If you set a Social Media allowance and feel covered, check what is actually in the bucket on your kid's phone before you trust it.
- Our position, said once and without a pitch. This is why BreakOff blocks by app rather than by category. It is also a worse user experience: you have to go and pick the apps yourself, and nobody enjoys that. We think the tradeoff is worth it. State it and stop.

**Hot take:** Apple built the best version of category-based screen limits anyone has shipped and then wired the categories to a checkbox the app's own team ticks. We filled this form in. It is not a hard form. It asks whether your app redistributes user-generated content through a feed, and if you say yes you take a 13+ rating, a Social Media badge and a seat in the bucket parents are about to start capping. Nobody at a company with a growth target reads that question neutrally. The honest version of a category system is that it is a map drawn by the territory. It will mostly be right, which is the problem, because mostly-right is exactly the confidence level at which a parent stops checking.

## Rank #3: Turning off notifications does not work either

**Why it ranks third:** Genuinely good research and a satisfying counterintuitive finding, but it ranks third because it is close to `/blog/do-app-blockers-work-research/` in shape. That post said opt-in blocking does not work. This one says notifications-off and time limits do not work. Two "the thing you tried does not work" posts in three weeks risks turning the blog into a wall of debunking, which is a bad look for a company that sells a tool. It is worth writing, but it needs the constructive half to be the bigger half.

**Adjacency warning for the writer:** do not re-explain the Marotta and Acquisti experiment. Link to the blocker post in one sentence and spend the words on the new material. If the draft starts to read like part two, it is wrong.

**Suggested title:** You Turned Off Your Notifications. Your Screen Time Did Not Move.

**Key points to cover:**

- The finding. Cynthia Dekker and colleagues at the University of Amsterdam tested the standard advice. Disabling notifications did not reduce screen time and did not reduce how excessive people felt their own use was. It also raised FOMO, and people checked their phones about as often as before. Time limits fared no better on either measure.
- Why it fails, in one line: notifications are the pull, but by the time a habit is established you are the pull. Removing the buzzer from a door you were already opening every ten minutes changes very little.
- What did work, which is the interesting half. Switching off TikTok's personalised algorithm. Participants stopped seeing only what matched their interests and started running into everything else. The intervention that worked was not on the person, it was on the feed.
- The honest caveat, do not cut it. Most people cannot switch off a personalised algorithm. That was a research condition, not a settings toggle. So the practical takeaway is not "turn off your algorithm", it is that the interventions available to you as a user are mostly the weak ones, and the strong ones live on the other side of a company's business model.
- The constructive close, and this needs to be the longest section. What survives all of this evidence is friction that is hard to undo and specific to a moment you have already identified as a problem. Not a system-wide settings change. One window, one commitment, an off switch you cannot reach. Point at the blocker research post once for the mechanism and let it carry the rest.

**Sources:**

- Folia (UvA), turning off notifications does not help reduce excessive screen use: https://www.folia.nl/en/wetenschap/174797/uva-study-turning-off-notifications-does-not-help-reduce-excessive-screen-use
- Dekker, Sumter and Baumgartner, perceived smartphone overuse and disconnection strategies, longitudinal, New Media and Society 2026: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/14614448241303113
- Dekker et al., Beyond the Buzz: notification-disabling intervention on smartphone behaviour and digital wellbeing: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/15213269.2024.2334025
- Full text PDF of Beyond the Buzz: https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/bitstream/handle/fub188/43549/Beyond%20the%20Buzz%20Investigating%20the%20Effects%20of%20a%20Notification-Disabling%20Intervention%20on%20Smartphone%20Behavior%20and%20Digital%20Well-Being.pdf
- Frontiers in Psychology, digital addiction and adolescent subjective wellbeing meta-analysis, 17 studies, 30,915 adolescents: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1776619/full
- PubMed record for the same meta-analysis: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41867976/
- Physician's Weekly summary of the meta-analysis: https://www.physiciansweekly.com/post/meta-analysis-links-digital-addiction-to-lower-well-being-in-adolescents

**Hot take:** Turning off notifications is the digital wellness equivalent of buying a gym bag. It feels like the first step and it is actually the whole thing, performed once, and then never followed by anything. The research keeps landing in the same place and it is not a comfortable place for anyone selling a solution, us included. Small self-administered changes do very little. What moved the needle in this study was changing the feed itself, which no user can do. So we are all left choosing between advice that is available and useless, and advice that works and is not on offer. The only thing left in the middle is a commitment your future self cannot talk their way out of, and the reason nobody markets that is that it does not feel good in the app store screenshot.
