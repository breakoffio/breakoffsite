## What moved this week

- **iOS 27 shipped on 9/14 and Time Allowances are now live.** Apple's newsroom post went up Monday. Time Allowances cap time across Entertainment, Games and Social Media as whole categories, with age-tailored default suggestions built with the American Academy of Pediatrics. There is a redesigned Screen Time with one-tap controls, a simpler setup flow for new child accounts, expanded Communication Safety that now blocks gore and violence including in live FaceTime, and Ask to Browse. Everything requires every device in the Family Sharing group to be on 27. This is the biggest platform event of the quarter and it converts a lot of our June and September speculation into shipped fact.
- **Ask to Browse is the sleeper feature and nobody is writing about it properly.** It flips a child's Safari from open-by-default to closed-by-default. Every new site is blocked until a parent approves it, the request arrives in Messages, and the parent can allow once, allow always or deny, and revoke later without touching the kid's device. The coverage is all how-to explainers. Nobody has said the obvious thing, which is that Apple just shipped default-deny for children and left every adult on default-allow. That is rank #2.
- **A peer-reviewed paper on chatbot addiction exists now, and it is good.** "The AI Genie Phenomenon and Three Types of AI Chatbot Addiction," CHI 2026, out of the Socius lab at UBC. Thematic analysis of 334 Reddit posts across 14 subreddits where people describe compulsive chatbot use. Three types: Escapist Roleplay, Pseudosocial Companion, Epistemic Rabbit Hole. Symptoms map onto the addiction literature, including inability to stop thinking about it, distress on quitting, and damage to work and relationships. This is the citation the rank #1 piece was missing on 9/2.
- **Google is moving in the same direction on Android.** Pause Point reworks app timers to be set in the moment rather than in advance, there is a Gemini Nano powered Weekly Wrap in development, and Android Parental Controls are expanding to every device that updates to Android 17. Not a post on its own, but worth a line in rank #2 because it shows both platforms converging on the same model.

**Suggested title:** Your App Blocker Has No Idea ChatGPT Exists

**Key points to cover:**

1. Open on the shipped fact, not the stat. iOS 27 landed on 9/14 with Time Allowances, the flagship screen time feature of the year, capping Entertainment, Games and Social Media. Three categories. A chatbot is filed under Productivity or Utilities and walks straight past all three. Tell the reader to go and check their own Screen Time settings right now. The verifiability is the hook.
2. Establish that this is a real behaviour and not a hypothetical, using the CHI paper. Three patterns, named: escapist roleplay, pseudosocial companion, epistemic rabbit hole. That third one is the one our readers will recognise in themselves, because it is the two hours that felt like research. Use the symptom list, which is inability to stop thinking about it, distress when trying to stop, and knock-on damage to work and relationships. Be careful with this source, see the accuracy note below.
3. Explain why the category system cannot fix this by itself. Social media announced itself as leisure, so you could catch yourself doing it. A chatbot hands you a small sense of accomplishment every few minutes and files itself under work. Every defence we have built as an industry, including ours, assumes the user already feels vaguely guilty. This one removes the guilt, which is the part that used to do the work.
4. Land on what actually helps, and keep it honest. The answer is not a new category. It is picking the specific apps that eat your evening rather than trusting a bucket someone else drew, and committing to it before you want to break it. That is our product argument and it should be one short paragraph near the end, not a pitch woven through the whole post.

**Accuracy notes, please read before writing:**

- **On the CHI paper, do not overclaim.** It is a thematic analysis of 334 Reddit posts from people who chose to write publicly about struggling with chatbot use. That is a self-selected sample, it is qualitative, and the authors do not claim prevalence. AI addiction is not a clinical diagnosis. Write it as "researchers documented three recognisable patterns," never as "X percent of users are addicted." The paper is strong enough on its own terms. It does not need inflating and inflating it is exactly how we would get caught.
- **On the usage numbers, cite OpenAI or skip it.** Our 9/2 brief said 1 billion weekly users confirmed on 7/31. The stats-aggregator sites we found this week say August 2026 instead. We could not reconcile the two from primary sources in this sweep. If you cannot find an OpenAI announcement stating the figure and the date, say "roughly a billion people a week" without a precise date, or leave the number out entirely. The argument does not depend on it. Do not cite backlinko, demandsage or similar SEO stat pages as the source for a headline number, because they are the reason the discrepancy exists.

**Hot take:** We all agreed the enemy was the feed, so we built our defences around the feed. While we were doing that, the thing that actually eats the evening started calling itself a productivity tool. Nobody feels guilty about forty minutes with a chatbot. That is the whole trick. Social media at least had the decency to look like leisure, so you could catch yourself at it. This one hands you a small sense of accomplishment every few minutes and never asks for anything back, which is a better hook than infinite scroll ever was. Apple shipped its best screen time feature ever on Monday and did not make a category for it, and we cannot really blame them, because none of us in this industry have either. The researchers who looked at what people actually say about this found people describing an inability to stop, distress when they tried, and work and relationships taking the hit. That is the vocabulary we spent a decade building for slot machines. It is being used about a text box. And the reason we are all going to be slow to say so out loud is that the entire industry that would normally sound the alarm, us very much included, is currently building on top of it.

## Rank #2: Apple gave children a default-deny internet and left the rest of us on default-allow

**Why it ranks second:** This is the most original angle available on the biggest news of the week, and it is genuinely unwritten. Every piece on Ask to Browse so far is a how-to with screenshots. None of them notice that Apple just picked a side in the oldest argument in our category, which is whether the sane default is open with exceptions or closed with exceptions, and picked closed for kids while leaving every adult on open. It ranks second rather than first only because the ChatGPT piece has waited three cycles and has a better SEO position. This one is timely and it will keep for a week, though not much more than that. If rank #1 fails for any reason, this is safe to auto-publish in its place.

**Adjacency warning for the writer:** `/blog/ios-27-screen-time-changes/` already covered what is in iOS 27 and `/blog/apple-screen-time-loophole-app-categories/` covered categories and labelling. This post is not a feature roundup and must not turn into one. Give the reader two sentences on what shipped, link the old post, and spend the rest on the default-deny argument. Also do not turn this into a parenting post. Our readers are mostly adults managing themselves, and the payoff is that the tool built for an eleven year old is better than the one built for them.

**Suggested title:** Apple Built a Default-Deny Internet. For Children.

**Key points to cover:**

1. What Ask to Browse actually does, briefly and accurately. A child's Safari goes closed by default. New site is blocked, the child taps to request, the request lands in the parent's Messages, the parent allows once, allows always or denies, and can revoke later without ever holding the child's phone. Works across iPhone, iPad and Mac inside a Family Sharing group.
2. Name the design decision. Every blocker on the market, ours included, is a blocklist. You name the bad thing in advance, which means you have to have already lost to it once, and anything new gets a free pass until you notice. Ask to Browse is an allowlist. The burden flips. This is the harder, better model and it is also the one people say they hate until they try it.
3. The honest objection, and do include it. Default-deny is friction, friction is a tax, and an approval queue with a parent at the other end does not scale to an adult managing themselves. Say so plainly. Then make the actual point, which is that commitment devices are the adult version of that same queue, because the whole idea is to put a small unavoidable gap between wanting and getting.
4. One short paragraph on Google doing the same thing from the other direction. Pause Point moves app timers to the moment of use rather than ahead of time, and Android Parental Controls are expanding to everything on Android 17. Both platforms are converging on in-the-moment gates. That is the industry telling on itself about what works.

**Hot take:** Apple looked at the open internet, decided it was not a reasonable default for a child, and shipped a browser where every new site is guilty until a grown-up says otherwise. Then it looked at you, a person with a documented four hour a day problem and nobody checking, and left everything switched on. The gap between those two decisions is the whole product category. We keep building tools for adults that assume the adult is fine and only needs better information, and then we send them a weekly report about how not fine they are. The eleven year old gets a gate. You get a graph. Here is the part that stings: almost everyone who tries default-deny for a week says the same thing, which is that they stopped noticing it by day three and the requests they never bothered to make were the ones they did not actually want. The friction is not the cost. The friction is the product. We just cannot sell it to adults because no adult wants to admit they need the same thing we gave the kid.

## Rank #3: iOS 27 lets apps decide whether they count as social media

**Why it ranks third:** Carried from last week's rank #2, unchanged in substance and now shipped, which helps it. It is still the piece where we have the most standing, because we are a developer who fills in this questionnaire. It drops to third this week for one reason, which is that it sits directly downstream of rank #1 and rank #2 and running three Apple category posts in a row would be a lot. It also needs care. The argument is subtle and if the writer gets it slightly wrong it reads as a cheap shot at Apple, which is not what we think and not a good look. It is still worth writing and it should not be carried a fourth time. If it does not rank top two on 9/23, we write it by hand or drop it.

**Adjacency warning for the writer:** `/blog/apple-screen-time-loophole-app-categories/` already covered Apple sorting apps into Social, Entertainment, Games and Other. Do not rewrite it. The new fact is that with Time Allowances now live, the bucket an app lands in has real consequences for real users, and the developer's own answers on the App Store Connect questionnaire help decide that bucket. That is the piece. Do not write it as an Apple hit job. The questionnaire is a reasonable thing for Apple to do and more honest than an algorithm guessing. The point is what it depends on.

**Suggested title:** iOS 27 Lets Apps Decide Whether They Count as Social Media

**Key points to cover:**

1. The mechanism, stated carefully. The App Store Connect age rating questionnaire now asks about social features. Declaring that your app surfaces user-generated content through a feed carries consequences, including a higher minimum age rating and a Social Media descriptor on the product page. Time Allowances went live on 9/14 and cap by category. Those two facts now touch each other.
2. The incentive problem, stated without accusing anyone of anything. Nobody at a company with a growth target reads that question neutrally. That is not fraud and the piece should not imply it is. It is what happens when you ask an interested party to classify itself and attach a cost to one of the answers.
3. Our own position, because it is the thing nobody else can write. We answered this questionnaire. Say what that was like. This is the paragraph that makes the post ours instead of commentary.
4. The general lesson, which is the house argument. Any system where the category is decided by someone other than you is a system where your limits are only as good as their answers. That is true of Apple's buckets, it is true of every blocker that ships a preset list, and it is the reason we think per-app choices you make yourself beat categories somebody else drew.

**Verification note:** we have not re-confirmed the exact current wording or the exact rating consequences of the App Store Connect questionnaire this week, and we last looked in early September. Before this publishes, somebody needs to open App Store Connect and check the live questionnaire. Do not describe the form from memory or from our previous brief. If that check does not happen, drop point 1 to a general description and do not quote specifics.

**Hot take:** Apple built the best version of category-based screen limits anyone has shipped, and then wired the categories partly to a form the app's own team fills in about itself. We filled this form in. It is not a hard form. It asks, in effect, whether your app is the kind of thing a parent would want to cap, and one of the answers costs you something. The honest version of a category system is that it is a map drawn in part by the territory. It will mostly be right. Mostly-right is the problem, because mostly-right is exactly the confidence level at which a parent stops checking, and the whole value of a limit is that you stopped having to think about it.
