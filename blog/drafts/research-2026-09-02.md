## Rank #1: Your app blocker has no idea ChatGPT exists

**Why it ranks first:** Carried from last week's #2, where we said it should be an easy #1 this week. It is. The most original idea we have had in months, the strongest product argument, and the news got better: 1 billion weekly users confirmed, and Apple shipping category caps this month that do not have a category for this. Evergreen SEO with a search cluster that barely exists yet ("block ChatGPT iPhone", "ChatGPT screen time", "limit AI app usage"), which is the best kind, because we get to define it. Distinct from `/blog/teens-want-phone-help-adults-holdouts/` (demand for help) and from `/blog/ios-27-screen-time-changes/` (feature overview). Safe to auto-publish.

**Adjacency warning for the writer:** `/blog/apple-screen-time-loophole-app-categories/` already covered how Apple's category system leaks. Do not re-litigate that. Lead with AI as a new behaviour, cite the category gap in one paragraph, and move on. If the draft starts to read like a category post with AI examples, it is the wrong piece. Also do not turn this into an anti-AI piece. We use these tools. The point is that "useful" and "habit-forming" are not opposites, and our defences only cover one of them.

**Suggested title:** Your App Blocker Has No Idea ChatGPT Exists

**Key points to cover:**

- The usage curve, in habit terms. 1 billion weekly users confirmed 7/31. Roughly 80% direct traffic. 12 to 15 minute sessions, 2 to 3 a day, 25 to 37 minutes daily, and multiple trackers agree it is additive to social, not a replacement. That is not tool-shaped usage. That is early-social-shaped usage. Note the honest caveat: a lot of those minutes are work. That is exactly why nobody counts them.
- The blind spot. Every default category block sorts apps into buckets written before this existed. iOS 27 Time Allowances, shipping this month, cap Entertainment, Games and Social Media. A chatbot sits in Productivity. Someone who blocks Social and Entertainment and feels covered is not covered. Say plainly what BreakOff does here: block by app, not by category, and the user has to actually go add it. The honest version is more persuasive than a pitch.
- Regulation is doing our job for us, badly, and only for kids. California SB 243, in effect since 1/1/26, requires a break reminder every three hours by default for known minors on companion chatbots. Colorado HB 1263 bans variable-interval reward loops. Common Sense has 72% of teens having used an AI companion, 52% regularly. Minors get statutory friction. Adults get nothing.

While we were doing that, the thing that actually eats the evening started calling itself a productivity tool. Nobody feels guilty about 40 minutes with a chatbot. That is the whole trick. Social media at least had the decency to look like leisure, so you could catch yourself. This one hands you a small sense of accomplishment every few minutes and never asks for anything back, which is a better hook than the infinite scroll ever was. Apple just shipped category caps and did not make a category for it. We are all going to be slow to admit this, because the entire industry that would normally sound the alarm, us included, is currently building on top of it.

## Rank #2: France just banned social media for under-15s. The part nobody is talking about is the phones.

**Why it ranks second:** It is the live news story of the week. The ban took effect 9/1, it is the first in the EU, and it has no parental consent loophole, which is the thing Australia's critics said would sink theirs. It ranks second, not first, because we have already published Australia (`/blog/australia-teen-social-media-ban-lessons/`) and the UK curfew (`/blog/uk-social-media-curfew-teens/`), and a third "country bans thing" post is one too many unless the angle is different. The different angle is buried in the same law: France also banned phones in secondary schools, in the same bill, and it is not in the headline. That plus the two-stage rollout (new accounts 9/1, existing accounts verified or deleted 1/1/27) gives us something the wire copy does not have. Safe to auto-publish if #1 fails, but the writer must lead with the school-phone clause and the enforcement mechanics, not the ban itself.

**Suggested title:** France Banned Social Media for Under-15s. Read the Second Half of the Law.

**Key points to cover:**

- What actually happened, briefly. Parliament passed it 7/21. New accounts blocked from 9/1/26. Existing under-15 accounts must be verified or closed from 1/1/27. Two verification routes: ID through a trusted third party, or selfie age estimation, neither retaining full identity data. No parental consent exception. Encyclopedias and educational directories exempt.
- The school phone clause. Same bill bans phones in secondary schools. France already had a primary and middle school ban since 2018. This extends it to lycée. That is a bigger daily behaviour change for a 16 year old than the social media ban is for a 14 year old, and almost no coverage mentions it. Tie back to our phone-free-schools post lightly, one sentence.
- The pattern. Australia, UK, France, and now Chile and Argentina with bills moving. Every one of these draws the line at an age. None of them have anything for the adult who wrote the law and checks their phone 186 times a day. Our standard asymmetry point, but keep it to a paragraph.

**Hot take:** Every country that bans social media for kids does the same two things. It picks an age, and it picks a technology. Both are wrong on purpose, because a law needs a line. But the line is not where the problem is. The problem is the phone in the pocket, and France quietly admitted that by banning phones in schools in the same bill and then not putting it in the press release. Adults keep passing laws that describe our own habits and then exempting ourselves from them by birthday.

## Rank #3: 37 students describe phone addiction in their own words

**Why it ranks third:** Carried for the third week, still unwritten, still good. Peer-reviewed 2026 research, qualitative, which is rare in this space and gives us quotes instead of another stat dump. The paper's own title is already a headline. It ranks third because the search cluster is soft and it is a "here is a study" piece rather than an argument. Clear of the published set: the EEG piece was neuroimaging, the two-week detox piece was recovery, the parents piece was adults with kids. Nothing we have covers young adults describing the experience from inside. If it does not rank in the top two next week either, we should either write it manually or retire it.

**Suggested title:** "I Can Never Be in a Room Alone and Not Be on My Phone"

**Key points to cover:**

- The design and why it matters. Eight focus groups, 37 students aged 18 to 22, Frontiers in Psychology, 8/3/26. Most screen time research counts things. This one asked people what it feels like.
- The tension the paper found. Phones both support and undermine wellbeing, often in the same behaviour. Resist writing it as a straight harms piece. The ambivalence is the finding.
- Optional pairing: the May 2026 Frontiers paper on the gap between perceived and objective smartphone use in adolescents. People do not know how much they use their phones, and the students in the focus groups are describing the feeling of that gap from the inside. One paragraph, not a second study section.

**Sources:**

- Frontiers in Psychology, "I can never be in a room alone and not be on my phone", 8/3/26: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1744046/full
- Frontiers in Computer Science, perceived vs objective smartphone use in adolescents, May 2026: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/computer-science/articles/10.3389/fcomp.2026.1773348/full
- Related 2026 Frontiers work on screen time and executive-motor function: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1694094/abstract

**Hot take:** We have spent ten years measuring this with a stopwatch, because a stopwatch is what the phone could give us. Hours export cleanly and chart nicely and tell you almost nothing. Ask a 20 year old what happens when they are alone in a room with no phone and you learn more in one sentence than a year of weekly reports will give you. The number was never the symptom. The flinch is.
