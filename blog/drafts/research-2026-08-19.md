## What moved this week

- **The best material we found is uncomfortable and it is about us.** Trends.vc published a category teardown of screen time blockers on August 5. The centerpiece is a randomized field experiment by Marotta and Acquisti. When blocking was imposed on people, they completed about 8 more tasks per hour and earned about 24% more per hour. When people were handed the same blocker and left to switch it on themselves, there was no significant change at all. The authors' phrase is that they "failed at effectively self-committing themselves." Opt-in blocking has a weak evidence base. Most of the category, including large parts of ours, is opt-in. We pulled the actual PDF and read the tables rather than trusting the summaries, which turned out to matter.
- **A fresh 2026 qualitative study is the sharpest thing we have read on how this actually feels.** Frontiers in Psychology ran eight focus groups with 37 students aged 18 to 22. They matched what people said against the six standard criteria for behavioural addiction. Five of the six were widely met. Most phone addiction research is surveys and screen time exports. This one is people describing it in their own words.
- **Android is quietly narrowing the plumbing every Android blocker runs on.** Android 17's Advanced Protection Mode blocks apps that are not classified as accessibility tools from the AccessibilityService API. Only screen readers, switch input, voice input and Braille tools qualify. Automation apps, launchers and monitoring tools do not. It is opt-in, not a blanket policy, but the direction is set. Android banking trojans jumped 56% in 2025 and nearly all of them abused this API, so Google has a good reason and no incentive to reverse it.
- **iOS keeps tightening in the other direction, in our favour.** Since iOS 26.4 a Screen Time passcode makes it much harder to strip Screen Time permissions without approval. iOS 27 this fall brings the redesigned Screen Time, Ask to Browse, Time Allowances by app category, and the Declared Age Range API. We already published the iOS 27 post. Plan a refresh when public release lands.
- **CHI 2026 published a leverage points framework for digital wellbeing** built on ten years of CHI papers. Useful for our own thinking about self-oriented versus systemic change. Too academic to be a post on its own.
- Carryover still unwritten: the teens-and-AI-coaching angle from 7/29, and the summer screen debt piece from 8/15. Both still fine. Neither is urgent.

## Rank #1: The research on app blockers is worse than our industry admits

**Why it ranks first:** This is the strongest piece of material we have found in months and nobody in our niche will touch it, because it makes the category look bad. That is exactly why we should write it. It is evergreen SEO on a fat query cluster ("do app blockers work", "why do screen time apps stop working", "best strict app blocker"). It is clear of everything published: the free blockers roundup is a list, the Opal comparison is a comparison, the pickups post is about metrics. None of them touch the mechanism question. And it lets us make our actual product argument without an ad, because BreakOff already ships the thing the evidence favours. Five modes ordered by how hard they are to exit, with Locked mode that cannot be ended early. We are not guessing at the conclusion. We built it first and found the research second. Safe to auto-publish.

**Suggested title:** Most App Blockers Do Not Work. The Research Is Pretty Clear About Why.

**Key points to cover:**

- The finding. Marotta and Acquisti ran a randomized field experiment on a crowdsourcing platform with real money on the line. Everyone got the same tool, Freedom. One group had it instrumented to block Facebook, Twitter, Instagram and YouTube on a fixed schedule, six hours a day, five days a week. The other group got the app and chose for themselves whether and when to block anything. The imposed group completed about 8 more tasks per hour, earned about $0.80 more per hour, and finished the key week with roughly 24% higher earnings per hour than the control group. The choose-for-yourself group showed no significant change on any measure. Same software, same people, same tasks. The only variable was who held the off switch.
- The detail that should end the argument. In the choose-for-yourself group, 30% installed the app and never used it a single time. Among the 70% who did use it, blocking averaged about 1.5 hours a day, and only about 10% of them ever scheduled a recurring session. The researchers then reran the analysis on just the people who actually used the app, in case the non-users were dragging the average down. Still nothing. It was not that some people ignored the tool. It was that using it on your own terms did not work either.
- Why this is damning for the category. Almost every screen time app on the App Store, ours included, is opt-in by default. You install it, you set it up, and every time you hit the wall you get to decide whether the wall stays. A blocker you can dismiss in two taps is not an intervention. It is a notification with extra steps. This is also why Apple and Google shipping free Screen Time and Digital Wellbeing did not kill this market. They built the version you can turn off, and people went out and paid for the version they cannot.
- The honest counterweight, and do not cut this. Forced blocking is not free. Mark, Iqbal and Czerwinski ran two weeks with 31 information workers using Freedom. Self-assessed productivity went up and focused immersion went up. But 16 of the 31 reported more stress, and the people who got more stressed scored significantly higher on impulsivity measures and reported feeling less in control of their work. Some of them stopped taking breaks at all and just ground through. Hard blocking works and it can also make you miserable, and it is hardest on exactly the people who need it most. Anyone selling it without saying that is selling you something.
- What we take from it. The variable that matters is not whether a block is hard, it is who decided and when. A block your past self chose is commitment. A block your employer chose is surveillance. This is the whole reason difficulty should be a dial and not a default. Pick flexible when you are just trying to be a bit more deliberate. Pick locked when you already know how tonight ends. The research says the strict end is where the effect lives. It also says do not start there.
- The practical close. Pick one recurring failure. Not your whole life, one window. Set the strictest mode you can honestly live with for that window only, and put the off switch somewhere your 11pm self cannot reach. Then leave the rest of your phone alone. Category-wide restriction is how people end up stressed and back where they started in nine days.

**Sources:**

- Marotta and Acquisti, "Online Distractions, Website Blockers, and Economic Productivity: A Randomized Field Experiment" (WEIS 2017) - https://weis2016.econinfosec.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/3/2017/06/WEIS_2017_paper_26.pdf
- Semantic Scholar entry for the same paper - https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Online-Distractions,-Website-Blockers,-and-Economic-Marotta-Acquisti/6e668a5914e30dd73429178da85bf5ba12b111c6
- Mark, Iqbal and Czerwinski, "How Blocking Distractions Affects Workplace Focus and Productivity" (UbiComp 2017) - https://interruptions.net/literature/Mark-UbiComp17.pdf
- "Some Middle School Students Want Behavior Commitment Devices (But Take-Up Does Not Affect Their Behavior)" - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5835880/
- Trends.vc category teardown, August 5 2026 - https://trends.vc/screen-time-blocking-apps-forced-versus-opt-in-the-licence-fence-lifetime-pricing/
- Fred Stutzman on paying to lock yourself out - https://www.newscientist.com/article/1969612-would-you-pay-to-block-your-own-internet-connection/

**Hot take:** Most of this category sells reassurance. You buy the app, you feel like someone who is doing something about it, and the doing-something feeling is the actual product. The blocking is a garnish, because you can dismiss it, and you do, roughly every night around eleven. We know this because we make one of these apps and we have watched the numbers. Here is the part nobody in our industry wants printed: the version that works is the version you will hate at least once a week. If your blocker has never genuinely annoyed you, it is not working, it is decorating. We would rather say that out loud and lose the sale to someone who wanted a nicer dashboard.

## Rank #2: 37 students describe phone addiction in their own words

**Why it ranks second:** Fresh 2026 peer-reviewed research, and it is qualitative, which is rare and gives us quotes instead of another stat dump. The title of the paper is already a headline. It ranks below #1 because the search cluster is softer and it is a "here is a study" piece rather than an argument. Clear of the published posts: the EEG piece was neuroimaging, the two-week detox piece was recovery, and the parents attachment piece was about adults with kids. Nothing we have covers young adults describing the experience from inside.

**Suggested title:** "I Can Never Be in a Room Alone and Not Be on My Phone"

**Key points to cover:**

- What they did and why it is different. Eight focus groups, 37 students aged 18 to 22, published in Frontiers in Psychology in 2026. Nearly all phone addiction research is surveys and screen time exports, which means we mostly know how long people scroll and almost nothing about what is happening while they do it. These researchers just asked and listened.
- The finding that matters. They matched what people said against the six established criteria for behavioural addiction. Five of the six were widely met. The word addiction gets thrown around loosely, usually by people selling something. This is the rare case where researchers tested the label properly and it held up.
- The counterintuitive bit. The researchers concluded that the phone being multifunctional does not explain the addiction. The usual story is that the phone is your camera and map and wallet and TV, so of course you cannot put it down. That story does not survive contact with the data. Something else is doing the pulling.
- The line to sit with. A room alone with no phone is now an unusual experience for a lot of people under 25. Not an unpleasant one. An unfamiliar one. That is a different problem than "too many hours" and it needs a different fix.

**Sources:**

- "I can never be in a room alone and not be on my phone." Understanding smartphone addiction of young adults, Frontiers in Psychology 2026 - https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1744046/full
- Addiction/Recovery eBulletin coverage - https://addictionrecoveryebulletin.org/smartphone-addiction-of-young-adults-i-can-never-be-in-a-room-alone-and-not-be-on-my-phone/
- "What is Digital Wellbeing? A Leverage Points Framework to Guide Research and Action", CHI 2026 - https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3772318.3793192
- Frontiers in Psychology 2026, screen time and executive-motor function in middle school students - https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2026.1694094/full

**Hot take:** We have spent ten years measuring this thing with a stopwatch because a stopwatch was what the phone could give us. Hours are easy to export and easy to chart and they tell you almost nothing. Ask a 20 year old what happens when they are alone in a room with no phone and you learn more in one sentence than a year of weekly reports will give you. The number was never the symptom. The flinch is.

## Rank #3: Android is closing the door your app blocker walks through

**Why it ranks third:** Genuinely useful, genuinely under-covered, and we can write it with more authority than any content farm because we ship on this platform. It ranks third because the search cluster is narrow and it partly explains why a competitor's product is worse rather than why ours is better. Also worth flagging: BreakOff is iOS only, so this is a piece about someone else's platform and we should write it straight rather than as a dunk.

**Suggested title:** Why Your Android App Blocker Keeps Getting Weaker

**Key points to cover:**

- The mechanism nobody explains. Android blockers do not have a real blocking API. They lean on the AccessibilityService API, which exists for screen readers and switch input, and they use it to watch what you open and slam a screen in front of it. It has always been a workaround.
- What changed. Android 17's Advanced Protection Mode stops apps that are not classified as accessibility tools from using that API. Google's definition is narrow: screen readers, switch input, voice input, Braille. Automation tools, launchers, monitoring apps and cleaners are explicitly out. It is opt-in today. Nobody should bet on it staying that way.
- Why Google is right to do it. Android banking trojans rose 56% in 2025 and nearly all of them abused this exact API. The same door that lets a blocker see what app you opened lets malware read your banking screen. There is no version of this where Google leaves it wide open, and honestly there should not be.
- The iOS contrast, told honestly. Apple built purpose-made APIs for this: FamilyControls, ManagedSettings, DeviceActivity. They are gated behind an entitlement you have to apply for per bundle ID, and Apple wants a real parental control or digital wellbeing use case before approving it. Slower for developers, sturdier for users. Since iOS 26.4 a Screen Time passcode also makes permissions much harder to strip. Different tradeoff, not a free win.
- What a reader should actually do. On Android, check whether your blocker still works after any system update and prefer tools that do not depend on accessibility permissions. On either platform, the real lesson is that anything built on a workaround is one update from breaking, so do not build your entire routine on a single app's permissions.

- Heise, "Android 17: Google cracks down on Accessibility API abuse" - https://www.heise.de/en/news/Android-17-Google-cracks-down-on-Accessibility-API-abuse-11210703.html
- Google Play policy on the Accessibility API - https://support.google.com/googleplay/android-developer/answer/10964491

- A developer's guide to Apple's Screen Time APIs - https://medium.com/@juliusbrussee/a-developers-guide-to-apple-s-screen-time-apis-familycontrols-managedsettings-deviceactivity-e660147367d7

**Hot take:** Every Android app blocker is standing on a plank labelled "for screen readers only" and hoping nobody notices. Google is noticing, for good reasons that have nothing to do with us, and the plank is getting narrower every release. The uncomfortable thing is that the platforms are the only ones who can build a blocker that genuinely cannot be dismissed, and neither of them wants to, because a phone you cannot open is a phone you do not look at. We are all building self-control tools on top of companies whose revenue depends on you failing.
