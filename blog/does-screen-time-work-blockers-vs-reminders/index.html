Do Screen Time Apps Work? Blockers Beat Reminders

October 15, 2026 · 5 min read

Do screen time apps work? Partly. Apps that only show you a number or send a reminder do very little. Apps that block distractions and change your environment do more. In a 2024 study of workshop students, blocking tools were rated most useful and goal reminders least useful. So it depends on which kind you install.

We make an app blocker, so you should know where we sit. We think the research backs the blocking side. We also think it says some uncomfortable things about the whole category, ours included.

What did the researchers actually find?

A recent Inc. piece syndicated by The Star (Jelinda Montes, August 2026) pulls together work from two researchers, and it is worth reading in full.

The first is Ulrik Lyngs, co-founder of the Centre for Digital Habits at the University of Oxford. His group runs workshops that help students cut digital distractions, and a 2024 paper from that work (presented at the CHI conference) sorted tools into four buckets: apps that block or remove distractions, tracking platforms, goal reminders, and tools that make your goals more attractive. Blocking tools were the most useful to students. Goal reminders were the least useful.

The second is Laura Zimmermann, a marketing professor at IE Business School in Madrid. Her research found that tracking your screen time made people more aware of their usage, but did not necessarily reduce it. Design changes did better. Things like switching to grayscale or removing YouTube's recommended videos page had a more measurable impact.

Which type of screen time app works best?

Here is the pattern in one table.

Type of toolWhat the research says
BlockersMost useful to students in the 2024 CHI paper
TrackersMore awareness, not necessarily less use
Goal remindersLeast useful in the same paper
Design changes (grayscale, hiding recommendations)More measurable impact than tracking

The article also cites other studies finding that simple nudges and reminders do little to reduce screen time. A reminder asks you to be disciplined at the exact moment you are least disciplined. A block takes the decision away.

Why do reminders and trackers fail?

Because they depend on willpower, and willpower is what the phone is built to beat.

Scott Harvey, who launched the free app ScreenZen in 2021, put it simply in the article: "It's really environment versus willpower." He said he built a screen time app and still caught himself on his phone in bed. That is a founder admitting the app was not enough on its own.

We have written about the same idea before. A number on a dashboard is not a plan, which is why we think counting pickups beats counting hours. The short version

Screen time apps work when they change your environment, not when they ask nicely. Skip anything that only reports or reminds, and pick something that blocks. Then make it a shared habit so it sticks.

Try a break that is hard to talk yourself out of. Start a shared phone-free break with the people around you and let the timer do the work.
