Schools Handed Every Kid a Laptop. Now They Are Taking Them Back.

August 27, 2026 · 5 min read

For about fifteen years, the goal in American schools was one device per child. A Chromebook for every kid, a cart in every room, a login for every worksheet. That was the definition of a modern classroom, and districts spent enormous money getting there.

They are now quietly undoing it.

This is bigger than a few districts having a moment

Utah passed a law in July that prohibits one-to-one device ratios for kindergarten through third grade, with a carve-out for standardized testing. Los Angeles Unified, the second largest district in the country, approved a policy in June eliminating in-school screens for pre-K through first grade and capping them above that.

Then there is the long tail. Marietta City Schools in Georgia went "closed by default" in July. Pulaski County in Virginia is phasing devices down to fifth grade and up by 2029-30. Haysville, Kansas is dropping individual middle school Chromebooks. Galesburg, Illinois pulled one-to-one for grades two through eight starting this school year. Fleming County, Kentucky is moving away from routine classroom use. EdWeek covered another district dropping Chromebooks for K through 2 just last week.

Zoom out and the pattern is legislative, not anecdotal. As of late July, lawmakers in at least 17 states had introduced or debated bills on classroom screen time or school-issued devices, and six states had already enacted something. Iowa capped digital instruction for elementary students. Maine ordered a study of its own program.

Why it is happening, honestly

There are two reasons, and only one of them is about attention.

The first is that the results never showed up. Test scores stayed flat or fell across the laptop era. Maine is the long-run case here, because it ran a statewide one-to-one initiative for fifteen years and does not have the outcomes to justify it. Fortune has been tracking districts that reached the same conclusion on their own.

The second reason is money. Pandemic federal funding dried up, and the devices wear out in well under a decade. A district facing a replacement bill for ten thousand Chromebooks has a strong incentive to discover that pen and paper works fine for seven year olds.

We are not going to pretend this is a pure pedagogical awakening. Some of it is a budget line that found a good story to stand behind. The reversal is still real, and the reasoning still holds, but the honest version includes the invoice.

Nobody asked the kids

Here is the part we keep coming back to. Not one of those students chose this. The default was set for them, at scale, by an institution. And then the institution mailed the distraction home in a backpack and told parents to manage it.

We spent a decade calling that rollout progress. It was procurement. Districts bought screens because screens were purchasable and attention was not. The blunt version of the sentence is that we handed kids a slot machine and called it a textbook.

Now the same institutions are walking it back, and that should land differently than it does. Because those kids are getting an intervention. They are getting an adult with authority looking at what is on the device and deleting whatever does not earn its place. That is the thing you keep promising yourself you will do in January.

An adult with a four hour a day problem gets a weekly Screen Time notification and a shrug. A room full of eleven year olds is currently being managed better than most of us are managing ourselves.

What this means if you are not eleven

We wrote earlier this year about what happened when schools went phone-free, and the discipline numbers were hard to argue with. That fight was about personal phones. This one is about the devices the school handed out itself, which is exactly where the problem moved once parents started winning the first fight.

The transferable lesson is not "screens are bad." It is that defaults beat intentions, every time, and somebody else set yours. The apps on your phone are there because a company put them there and a category system decided they were fine. You did not audit that. Almost nobody does.

So borrow the method these districts are using. Closed by default, open on purpose. Decide what earns a place on the device rather than deciding what to remove when things get bad. Protect specific windows instead of setting a vague goal for the month. And put the friction somewhere your future self cannot casually undo it, because willpower at 11pm is not a plan.

The difference between you and a fourth grader in Galesburg is that no superintendent is going to walk your defaults back for you. That part is yours.
