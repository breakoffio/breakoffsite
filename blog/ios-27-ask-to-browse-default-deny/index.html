Apple Built a Default-Deny Internet. For Children.

September 24, 2026 · 4 min read

On September 14, Apple shipped Ask to Browse, one of several child safety features that landed in iOS 27. The idea is simple. A new website is blocked in Safari until a parent approves it.

That is the whole feature. Not "we will flag anything that looks sketchy." Not "here is a report at the end of the week." Every new site is closed by default, and a parent opens the door one site at a time.

We wrote about the categories Apple uses for adult Screen Time back in June, and about what iOS 27 was bringing before it shipped. This is not a rehash of either post. This is about the shape of the decision Apple made for an eleven year old, and the fact that nobody built anything like it for you.

The allowlist, not the blocklist

Almost every app blocker on the market, ours included, works the opposite way. You build a blocklist. You decide in advance which sites and apps are the problem, and you block those specifically. Which means the moment something new shows up, something you did not think to name, it walks right through, because you already lost to it once just by not knowing it existed.

Ask to Browse flips that. It is not a blocklist. It is an allowlist. Nothing gets in until somebody lets it in. The burden moves from "name the bad thing" to "approve the good thing," and that is a much harder door to walk through by accident.

The honest objection

Let's be honest about the catch, because it is a real one. Default deny is friction, genuine friction, and an approval queue with a parent on the other end does not translate to an adult managing themselves. You would be your own parent in that scenario, and asking yourself for permission through the same device that wants you to open the site is not a real gate. It is a suggestion.

But a commitment device is the adult version of that queue. You set the limit while you still agree with yourself, before you are standing in front of the thing you want, and future you hits a wall that present you built on purpose. It is a small gap between wanting something and getting it, placed there deliberately. Same shape Apple built for an eleven year old, just with no parent standing at the door. You have to be the parent.

Two platforms, one instinct

Apple is not the only platform doing this right now, either. Google's September Pixel Drop rolled a feature called Pause Point out to the Pixel 6 and newer on September 15, after it launched as a Pixel 11 exclusive back in August. Open a distracting app you have flagged and you wait about ten seconds first, with a "why am I here" style prompt and options like breathing or setting a session timer. Several outlets report that turning it off requires a restart. Two different platforms, two very different audiences, and the same instinct: put something between the hand and the app.

That convergence is the actual story here. Apple and Google did not compare notes. They both looked at unrestricted access to an app and decided that the decision has to happen at the door, not after the fact, if it is going to work. That is not a hunch or a marketing angle. It is the two biggest platforms on earth independently building the same shaped gate for two very different users.

The gap

The kid gets a gate. You get a weekly report you probably do not read. That gap is most of the reason our category exists at all. The friction is not a cost Apple is trying to minimize for its youngest users. The friction is the product, and Apple is confident enough in that to make it the default for millions of kids overnight.

We just have a much harder time selling that same idea to adults, because almost nobody wants to admit they need the exact thing Apple just decided an eleven year old could not be trusted without.
