Half of Us Say We Use Our Phones Too Much. Fixing It Mostly Lands on "Somewhat."

October 1, 2026 · 4 min read

Most of us already know we look at our phones too much. What we did not have was a clean, large-sample number for how many of us feel that way, and how it goes when we try to change it.

Now we do. And the part nobody is leading with is the second half.

What Pew found

The Pew Research Center surveyed 9,750 US adults between May 26 and June 1, 2026, and published the results on September 1. The headline numbers:

  • 53% say they spend too much time on their smartphone. That climbs to 70% of adults 18 to 29 and 64% of those 30 to 49.
  • 26% say they use their phone "almost constantly." Among 18 to 29 year olds, it is 42%.
  • 41% say their phone hurts their sleep. Only 5% say it helps.

That sleep split is the one we keep coming back to. Almost nobody thinks the phone is making their nights better, and yet it is the last thing most of us touch before bed. The survey was also covered by NPR.

The part about trying to fix it

Here is where it gets interesting. 45% of adults say they tried to cut back on their phone use in the past year. Among 18 to 49 year olds, it is 58%. So this is not a story about people who do not care.

And how did it go? Of the people who tried:

  • 25% say they were extremely or very successful.
  • 52% say they were somewhat successful.
  • 23% say they were not too or not at all successful.

We want to be careful here, because it is easy to spin this. It would be wrong to say most people failed. 77% call it at least somewhat successful. The honest read is narrower: only about one in four people who tried call it a real win.

Why "somewhat" is the word to watch

This next part is our take, not Pew's. Self-reported success is soft, and we are not saying anyone is exaggerating. We are saying that "somewhat successful" is exactly what a half-working plan feels like from the inside. You cut back a little. Some days it holds. Then a rough week hits and you are back at the same number.

That is not a willpower problem. You are going up against products built by thousands of engineers whose job is to make the next tap feel automatic. Fighting that with a note to self is bringing a sticky note to a data center. "Somewhat" is the phone winning on points.

We think you can do better than somewhat. But it takes a different plan than "use it less."

What tends to help

We are not going to invent numbers here. What the research we have covered points to is small and practical:

  • Pick the one or two apps that actually hurt. Total hours is a blurry target. We wrote about why in stop counting hours, count pickups.
  • Add a little friction to those apps. A short pause before opening is enough to break the reflex. We went through what the evidence says in do app blockers work.
  • Protect sleep first. With 41% saying their phone hurts it, an evening cutoff is the highest-return change most people can make.

And if you are wondering whether this is just a young person problem, it is worth reading how teens actually want help while adults hold out. The Pew numbers say adults feel it too.

The takeaway

Half of us say we are on our phones too much. A quarter of us are on them almost constantly. And the people who tried to fix it mostly landed on "somewhat."

That gap between trying and winning is the whole opportunity. Somewhat is a fine place to start. It is a bad place to stay.
