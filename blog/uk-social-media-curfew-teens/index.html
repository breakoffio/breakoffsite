The UK Just Put Social Media on a Curfew. Your Phone Should Have Had One Years Ago.

August 15, 2026 · 5 min read

For about a decade, the app blocker category has been quietly arguing one thing: the problem is not the content, it is the design. Infinite scroll. Autoplay. No natural stopping point at 1am.

Last month the UK government agreed, in writing, and proposed turning those arguments into national policy.

What was actually announced

On July 15 the UK proposed a default social media curfew for 16 and 17 year olds: a midnight to 6am lockout on Instagram, TikTok, and YouTube, with autoplay and infinite scrolling switched off by default for that age group. It was covered by CNBC, Variety, and Al Jazeera.

Two things to be clear about. First, it is voluntary in the sense that a teenager can turn it off. It is a default, not a wall. Second, it sits alongside a separate and much harder under-16 ban planned for 2027.

The trial the proposal leans on found teens slept better and concentrated better. Which, honestly, is not a shocking result. It turns out that if you close the casino at midnight, people sleep.

Defaults are quietly more powerful than bans

The reason this matters more than a ban headline is that defaults are the strongest behavioral lever anyone has. Most people never change a setting. Ever. That is why every dark pattern in the industry is a default and not a prompt.

Autoplay on by default is the reason you watched six videos. Infinite scroll on by default is the reason there was no moment where you decided to stop. Nobody chose those. They were set for us, and we lived inside them.

Flipping those defaults the other way is the whole game. We looked at this in our post on what Australia's teen social media ban actually produced after three months. Blanket bans push kids sideways into other apps. Design defaults change the shape of the time people already spend. The UK proposal is the second thing, and it is the more interesting one.

Regulators stopped asking nicely

The same week, Ofcom published its age assurance report. In the second half of 2025 there were 69 million age checks, a 23x increase, and social platforms are still failing to keep underage kids off. You can read the report from Ofcom directly.

That is the real story under the curfew headline. Verification alone did not work, so governments moved on to specifying the product itself. Not "please keep kids safe." Instead: turn off autoplay, break the scroll, lock the app at midnight. Regulators are now writing feature specs.

Reasonable people disagree about whether that is a good idea. Reason made the civil liberties case against it, and the concern is not silly: a government that can mandate a bedtime for an app can mandate other things about apps. We are not going to pretend that question is settled.

The part nobody is saying out loud

Here is what gets us. A 17 year old in the UK may soon get a state-mandated bedtime for TikTok, on the reasoning that the design of these apps overwhelms self-control and wrecks sleep.

Every one of those arguments applies to adults. US adults average 4 to 5 hours a day on their phones, global screen time is up around 6h38m across screens, and somewhere between 46% and 57% of Americans describe themselves as addicted to their phone. Downloads of screen time apps are up 30% year over year while actual screen time keeps climbing.

So the state thinks a teenager needs help closing the app at midnight, and you, at 1am, on your third hour of scrolling, get nothing. Not because you are fine. Because you are an adult and nobody is coming.

The upside is that you do not need a bill to pass. The curfew the UK is debating for months is a setting you can turn on tonight in about ninety seconds.

Build your own curfew

If you take one thing from the UK proposal, take the shape of it. It is not a detox. It is three defaults.

  • A hard overnight window. Midnight to 6am, or 10pm to 7am if you want the version that actually protects sleep. Scheduled, automatic, not a decision you make when tired.
  • Kill autoplay where you can. YouTube, TikTok, and Instagram all bury the toggle, but it exists. Every autoplay you turn off is one decision point handed back to you.
  • Put friction on the reflex, not the day. A pause screen before the app opens beats a daily hour budget, because the problem is rarely the hour you planned. It is the pickup you never noticed.

The evidence that this works is not vibes. A randomized trial published in BMC Medicine capped participants at two hours of phone use a day for three weeks and measured improvements in depression, stress, sleep, and overall wellbeing. Causal, not correlational. Enforced reduction moves the needle. Watching a chart of your usage does not.

The takeaway

Governments are now shipping the features app blockers invented. A midnight lockout and a broken infinite scroll is a screen time app with a flag on it.

The funny part is who gets it. Teenagers, by law. The adults who built and use these apps for five hours a day get to keep white-knuckling it on the honor system.

If the UK thinks a 17 year old needs a curfew to sleep, it is worth asking what your excuse is. You do not have to wait for a law. You just have to change a default.
