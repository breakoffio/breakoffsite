A Week Off Social Media Helped. Their Screen Time Didn't Drop.

October 2, 2026 · 4 min read

Everyone expected the same result. Take people off social media for a week, and they will put the phone down.

They did not. And they felt better anyway.

What the study did

A study in JAMA Network Open asked participants to step away from social media for a week. The detail that makes it worth trusting is that usage came from phone-logged data, not from people guessing how much they used.

What happened

Social media time fell from about 2 hours a day to about 30 minutes. Total screen time did not fall. It rose slightly. Meanwhile, symptoms moved:

  • Depression scores dropped 24.8%.
  • Anxiety dropped 16.1%.
  • Insomnia dropped 14.5%.
  • Loneliness did not move.

The coverage from Earth.com and the Harvard Gazette makes the same point we keep landing on: the picture is more nuanced than "less phone, better mood."

That last line is easy to skim past. Feeling less depressed and less anxious, while loneliness stays put, tells us a week off the feeds is not the same as a week of better relationships. It lifts a weight. It does not build anything on its own.

It also tells us something about where the damage comes from. If total screen time stayed flat and people still felt better, then the hours were not the problem. The feeds were. Whatever replaced them, whether that was messaging, reading, video or plain boredom, did not carry the same cost.

The limits, stated plainly

We do not want to oversell this. It was voluntary, not randomized. It lasted one week. Participants were paid. And most of the benefit went to people who started out struggling. This is a promising signal, not a cure.

Why the weekly number is the wrong number

Here is our take. People did not put the phone down. They stopped opening the feeds, moved their time somewhere else, and felt better anyway. So the total hours figure you check every Sunday may be measuring the wrong thing.

We made a related argument in stop counting hours, count pickups. This is the sequel. Hours are a blunt tool. What the phone is doing to you depends on which apps are doing it. If you want the broader evidence on cutting back, we covered it in reducing screen time and mental health.

What this means if you are not quitting anything

Most of us are not going to delete every social app for a week, and the study does not ask us to. The useful part is the shape of the result. The people who gained the most were the ones who started out struggling. If you already know which app leaves you feeling worse after you close it, you are probably the person this applies to.

That is a much smaller ask than a digital detox. You do not need a new lifestyle. You need to find the one or two apps that cost you something and make them a little harder to reach on autopilot. A short pause, a daily cap, a locked window at night. The study suggests that is where the leverage is.

What to do with it

Aim your limits at the one or two apps that hurt, not at your total. Ignore the screen time total for a week and watch your sleep and mood instead. If those improve while the total stays flat, the plan is working.
