40 phone-free family time ideas you can start tonight

Phone-free family time does not need to be a big production. The best ideas are small and repeatable. Here are 40 of them, sorted by when you are most likely to use them.

One tip before the list: decide on the phone part first, then the activity. The American Academy of Pediatrics suggests screen-free zones such as the dinner table, homework time, and before bed (HealthyChildren.org, AAP). Those make great starting points, because they already happen every day.

At the dinner table

  1. High, low, funny. Everyone shares the best part of their day, the hardest part, and one funny thing.
  2. The question jar. Fill a jar with questions ("What would you do with a free day?") and pull one per meal.
  3. Kid chooses the menu. Once a week, one kid picks dinner and helps cook it.
  4. Two truths and a lie. Works from about age six up, and parents are often the easiest to catch.
  5. Candle dinner. Lights down, candle on. It changes the whole mood.

Weeknight evenings (30 to 60 minutes)

  1. Walk the block. Same route every night. Kids start noticing what changes.
  2. One puzzle, all week. Leave a jigsaw out and let everyone chip away at it.
  3. Card game rotation. Uno, Go Fish, Spit, Rummy. Let a different person pick each night.
  4. Read-aloud chapter book. One chapter a night, even for kids who read on their own.
  5. Kitchen dance party. Three songs, full volume, while you tidy up.

Weekend mornings

  1. Pancake lab. Everyone invents a pancake topping combination and the family votes.
  2. Farmers market run. Give each kid a small budget to choose one thing.
  3. Library trip. Everyone leaves with a book for someone else in the family.
  4. Backyard or balcony project. A herb pot, a bird feeder, a sidewalk chalk mural.
  5. Slow breakfast. No plan, no rush. Just eat and talk until someone gets bored.

Weekend afternoons

  1. Local hike or park you have never tried. Let a kid pick from a map.
  2. Build a fort. Blankets, chairs, clothespins. Read in it afterward.
  3. Board game tournament. Bracket on a piece of paper, small prize for the winner.
  4. Bike ride with a snack stop. The snack is the destination.
  5. Family cleanup challenge. Set a timer for 20 minutes, then do something fun as a reward.

Car rides

  1. The alphabet game. Find each letter in order on signs and license plates.
  2. Twenty questions. Classic for a reason.
  3. Story chain. Each person adds one sentence to a story.
  4. Family playlist DJ. Each person gets to pick the next song, no skipping.
  5. "Would you rather." Keep it silly and it works for any age.

Bedtime wind-down

  1. Phones charge outside bedrooms. A shared charging spot in the kitchen, for everyone.
  2. Gratitude round. One thing each person was glad about today.
  3. Stretch or breathe together. Five minutes on the floor before lights out.
  4. Audiobook or story, no screen. A speaker in the room, lights low.
  5. Tomorrow planning. Five minutes to talk through tomorrow so nobody lies awake worrying.

Rainy days and snow days

  1. Bake something from a family recipe. Ask a grandparent for one.
  2. Homemade game show. Kids write the questions about the family.
  3. Art swap. Everyone draws a portrait of someone else.
  4. Indoor picnic. Blanket on the living room floor.
  5. Build something with cardboard. Boxes, tape, markers, and an hour.

Bigger traditions

  1. Monthly phone-free day. One Saturday a month, everyone unplugs until dinner.
  2. Family volunteer morning. A park cleanup or food bank shift.
  3. Backyard camp-out. Tent, flashlights, stories.
  4. Letter to your future family. Write one together, seal it, open it in a year.
  5. Teach-me night. Each person teaches the family a skill they know, from a card trick to a dance move.

How to make phone-free time actually stick

The ideas are the easy part. The hard part is the phones, which pull everyone back in, adults included. In a 2024 Pew Research Center survey, 47% of US parents and 38% of US teens said they spend too much time on their smartphones (Pew Research Center). Nobody is doing anything wrong. It is just hard to put down something that never runs out.

A few things that help:

  • Everyone does it, including parents. Kids are far more willing when the adults put their phones down too.
  • Same time, same place. A regular slot (every weeknight dinner, every Saturday morning) beats good intentions.
  • Count the wins, not the misses. Celebrate every phone-free hour. Skip the lectures when one does not happen.

Frequently asked questions

How do you start phone-free family time?

Pick one short, regular slot that already happens every day, like dinner, and make it phone-free for everyone, including parents. Put phones in one spot, choose a simple activity, and build from there once it feels normal.

How long should phone-free family time be?

There is no set rule. Many families start with a single meal or 30 minutes in the evening, then add longer blocks like a weekend morning once the habit is in place. Consistency matters more than length.

What can families do together without phones?

Cook a meal together, play card or board games, go for a walk or bike ride, read aloud, build a fort, or play car games like twenty questions. The list above has 40 ideas sorted by time of day.

How do I get my teenager to put their phone down?

Put yours down at the same time, and give them a say in when and how. Shared rules land better than one-way ones. An app like BreakOff can start a break on everyone's phone at once so it feels fair.
